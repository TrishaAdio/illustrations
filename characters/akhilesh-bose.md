# অখিলেশ বোস — Akhilesh Bose

Series working title: **সময়সারণি** / *The Timetable*

---

## The one-line

A retired railway signal engineer in a small junction town solves crimes by
noticing what ran late.

## Why not another detective

The temptation is to write a young, tall, brilliant investigator with a teenage
narrator and a comic sidekick. That road is taken, and taken definitively. So
Akhilesh Bose is built by inverting nearly every axis of it:

| | the familiar model | Akhilesh Bose |
|---|---|---|
| age | late twenties | sixty-one |
| build | tall, athletic | short, heavy-shouldered, a limp |
| trade | professional investigator | retired railway servant, no licence, no fee |
| method | observation of physical detail | deviation from schedule |
| base | Kolkata, travels outward | one small town, everything comes to him |
| narrator | admiring young cousin | an impatient young woman who thinks he is slow |
| register | brisk, confident | slow, courteous, faintly obstinate |

He is not brilliant. He is **patient**, and he has been paying attention to the
same square mile for thirty-nine years.

## The method

Akhilesh spent his working life on the signal box at Ghatanpur Junction, where a
mistake of ninety seconds kills people. He came out of it believing that
everything has a timetable — not only trains.

> The 4:30 goods. The milkman's second round. The temple bell. The hour the
> crows quit the peepul by the water tank. When Nikunja-babu coughs on his
> verandah. The Tuesday bus that is always eleven minutes late and never twelve.

A crime, to him, is not a puzzle of clues. It is a **deviation from schedule**.
Somebody was somewhere at the wrong minute, or something that should have made a
noise did not. He does not search for evidence; he reconstructs the day's
timetable and looks for the entry that does not fit.

His line, delivered without drama, usually while looking at something else:

> **সময়টা মিলছে না।**
> *The timing doesn't match.*

This is a genuinely different engine from deduction-by-detail, and it has a
useful narrative property: it makes the *town* the real subject. To solve
anything he must know who does what, when — so every case is an excuse to
inventory a community.

## Physical

Built to be identifiable in silhouette at thumbnail size, because he has to be
drawn repeatedly and consistently:

- **Short and wide.** Barely five foot four, heavy through the shoulders, thick
  forearms from thirty-nine years of pulling signal levers.
- **Bald across the crown**, coarse white hair at the sides standing out above
  the ears. Short white moustache, no beard.
- **Round steel-rimmed spectacles**, thick lenses. The left lens has a hairline
  crack he has refused to replace since 1959.
- A **grey-brown alpaca coat**, shabby but pressed, worn over a dhoti in all
  weather including June.
- A **black chador** over the left shoulder in cold months.
- A **railway pocket watch** on a brass chain — Eastern Railway issue, his
  retirement gift, and the only thing he is vain about. He checks it constantly,
  not to know the time but to think.
- **Limps on the left leg** — a shunting accident, 1958, which is also why he
  retired at fifty-two rather than fifty-eight.
- Carries a **black umbrella as a walking stick** and refuses to open it in
  rain, which the town finds either saintly or idiotic.

Silhouette signature: **squat mass, round head, round glasses, umbrella.**

## Interior

**What he is:** courteous to the point of obstruction. Slow in speech. Kind in a
way that inconveniences him. Physically brave in a plodding manner, having
already been under a wagon once.

**Flaws, real ones:**
- He hoards paper. Every timetable, ticket stub, receipt and gazette since 1952
  is in the back room, in order. His sister-in-law calls it a fire hazard. It is
  also, twice a book, the thing that solves the case — which he is insufferable
  about.
- He cannot bear to be interrupted mid-sentence, and will simply start the
  sentence again from the beginning. Every time.
- He never learned to swim, and moving water frightens him badly. In riverine
  Bengal this is a serious handicap and he is ashamed of it.
- He was a **mediocre signal engineer** — passed over for promotion three times.
  He knows exactly why: he was too slow to be trusted with a busy box. The thing
  that makes him good at this made him bad at that.

**What he wants:** to be of use after being told, at fifty-two, that he no
longer is.

## The people

**Mitali Sen — "Mitul"**, 24. The new post-mistress, two years out of Bethune
College, sent to Ghatanpur as a punishment posting she is determined to convert
into an escape. Rides a second-hand Rajdoot motorcycle, which scandalises the
town. Impatient, sharp, funnier than he is. She is not his assistant and does
not admire him; she finds him maddeningly slow and uses him, initially, as a
curiosity to write letters home about. She has what he lacks: **the mail** —
which is to say, information, and the whole district's business passing through
her hands. He has what she lacks: thirty-nine years of memory. The books are as
much hers as his.

**Inspector Nirmal Dhar**, 40s. Ghatanpur thana. Competent, ambitious, posted
somewhere that cannot advance him, and correctly aware that an unlicensed
sixty-one-year-old solving his cases is humiliating. Not corrupt, not stupid —
which makes him a better antagonist than a villain would be. Their working
relationship is a long negotiation over credit.

**Sarojini**, his widowed sister-in-law, who keeps his house, disapproves of the
paper, and is the only person permitted to interrupt him.

**Bhondu**, a three-legged street dog who meets the 4:30 goods train every day
without exception. When Bhondu is not at the platform, something is wrong. The
dog is, functionally, a clock.

## Setting

**Ghatanpur Junction**, a small town on a branch line some hours out of
Kolkata — invented, so no real town has crimes attributed to it. A junction
matters: two lines meet, so strangers pass through legitimately, and a stranger
in a mofussil town is otherwise implausible. It gives an endless supply of
outsiders without contrivance.

**1963.** Chosen deliberately:
- Steam still runs on the branch lines — visually magnificent, and smoke, steam
  and soot are gifts to a black-ink illustration style.
- Transistor radios and telephone trunk calls are arriving, unevenly. Modernity
  is present but not yet decisive.
- Before 1971 and before the Naxal years, so the politics can stay in the
  background rather than swallowing the books.

## Recurring visual motifs

For consistency across illustrated plates:

- semaphore signal arms against sky, up or down
- the pocket watch, open, in a thick hand
- rails converging, and a level crossing gate
- the back room: paper stacked to the ceiling
- steam and smoke as flat black masses
- Mitul's motorcycle leaning outside the post office
- a wet tin roof, rain
- Bhondu on the platform, or Bhondu absent from it

## Voice

Third person, close on Akhilesh, past tense. Plain sentences. The prose should
be as unhurried as he is and should never explain his conclusion before he does.
Bengali dialogue in Kolkata register; he speaks formally, uses সাধু constructions
when nervous, and calls almost everyone আপনি including children.

Mitul's dialogue is faster, more colloquial, and cuts across his.

## First case, sketched

**সাড়ে চারটার মালগাড়ি** / *The 4:30 Goods*

The dog is not on the platform. Akhilesh notices this before anyone notices the
body, and spends the book insisting the two facts are one fact. A man comes back
to Ghatanpur after seventeen years, on a wet evening in June, and nobody has
come to meet him — which is itself the deviation, since the town knew he was
coming.

That opening already exists in draft:

> বর্ষার প্রথম দিনে রেলগাড়িটা যখন স্টেশনে থামল, অমল দেখল প্ল্যাটফর্মে কেউ নেই।
> শুধু ভেজা টিনের ছাদ থেকে জল গড়িয়ে পড়ছে, আর দূরে কোথাও একটা কুকুর কাঁদছে।
> সতেরো বছর পরে সে ফিরে এসেছে, কিন্তু গ্রামটা যেন তার ফেরার খবর পায়নি।

The returning man is Amal. He is not the detective, and he is not innocent.
